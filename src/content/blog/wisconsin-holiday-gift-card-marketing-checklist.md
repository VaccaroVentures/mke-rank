Wisconsin businesses should start holiday gift card marketing in early November, then increase visibility through Thanksgiving weekend and December. Before promoting anything, make sure a customer can buy, receive, give, and redeem a card without staff improvising. Publish the purchase link in the places local customers already check, state the important terms clearly, test every automated message, and plan a January redemption follow-up before the first card is sold.

The timing is already relevant. In the [Google Trends view for “gift cards” in Wisconsin over the past five years](https://trends.google.com/trends/explore?date=today%205-y&geo=US-WI&q=gift%20cards), reviewed November 8, 2026, normalized weekly interest rose from 18 for October 25–31 to 26 for November 1–7; the current partial week was 27. Each prior full year reached its peak during December 17–28: 86 in 2021, 80 in 2022, 89 in 2023, 88 in 2024, and 100 in 2025. Google Trends measures relative interest within the selected place and period—not search volume, sales, or a forecast. The useful signal is the recurring holiday pattern and the current November rise.

Wisconsin businesses are already acting on that calendar. Seven Acre Dairy Company began its [2026 holiday gift card promotion on November 5](https://sevenacredairyco.com/dine/holidaygiftcard/), with different bonus terms for November 5–11 and November 12–30. That is one current example, not a benchmark or proof that the same offer will work for every shop.

## The short answer: launch only after the full gift-card journey passes a test

A gift card is a promise that crosses marketing, payment, delivery, bookkeeping, customer service, and future fulfillment. Run one controlled purchase before sending a campaign:

1. Buy a low-value test card through the same page customers will use.
2. Confirm the payment receipt and gift-card email arrive promptly.
3. Open every link on a phone and a computer.
4. Check the sender name, subject line, branding, amount, code, and instructions.
5. Schedule a future delivery if the platform offers it.
6. Redeem part or all of the test value in the actual point-of-sale or booking flow.
7. Confirm the balance and transaction record update correctly.
8. Ask an employee who did not configure the system to explain the process back to you.

If any step depends on a manager remembering a workaround, fix or document it before promotion. A larger audience will not make a broken fulfillment path easier to manage.

## Use a four-phase holiday gift card calendar

The right dates depend on the business and its buying cycle, but this sequence gives a Wisconsin local business a practical starting point.

### Phase 1: prepare and softly launch in early November

Use the first half of November to finalize the product, terms, creative, tracking, and staff instructions. Add a permanent gift-card link to the website navigation or another obvious page, then announce availability to existing customers without manufactured urgency.

This phase is for finding friction. Watch for failed payments, delayed emails, confused replies, and redemption questions. Correct the path while volume is manageable.

### Phase 2: build visibility before Thanksgiving weekend

By the week before Thanksgiving, the purchase link should appear consistently on the website, Google Business Profile, email, social profiles, receipts, and in-store signage where applicable. Keep the destination the same so staff and customers are not choosing between competing checkout links.

This article complements the [Wisconsin Small Business Saturday marketing checklist](/blog/wisconsin-small-business-saturday-marketing-checklist/), but the campaigns have different jobs. Small Business Saturday is a short event window; gift cards can remain useful through late December and create a second customer visit later.

### Phase 3: increase frequency in December without changing the rules

December is when the previous Google Trends peaks occurred. Increase reminders as the holiday approaches, but resist changing the offer every few days. Repeatedly communicate the same essentials:

- who the gift card is for;
- what it can be used to purchase;
- whether it is physical, electronic, or both;
- how and when it is delivered;
- where it can be redeemed;
- the stated terms for any separate promotional bonus;
- how a customer gets help.

E-gift cards can solve a late-shipping problem, but only if delivery is reliable and the buyer understands whether the email goes to them or directly to the recipient.

### Phase 4: switch from selling to redemption support after the holiday

Do not let the campaign end at purchase. In late December and January, update the website and staff script to emphasize redemption, booking, balance questions, and the next step after a first visit. If a separate promotional certificate has a valid-use window, communicate that window exactly as issued.

Redemption is a service moment, not merely an accounting event. A recipient may be meeting the business for the first time. Make the booking, checkout, and remaining-balance experience as clear as the original sale.

## Choose an offer the business can explain in one sentence

The simplest campaign may be “Holiday gift cards are available online and in store.” A bonus can add urgency, but it also adds terms, accounting, staff training, and customer questions.

Before using “buy $100, receive a $20 bonus,” decide and document:

- whether the purchased card and promotional bonus are separate instruments;
- who receives each one;
- the purchase dates that qualify;
- the bonus redemption dates;
- excluded products, services, fees, or gratuities;
- whether the bonus can be combined with other offers;
- what happens with refunds or partial redemptions;
- how lost physical cards or failed emails are handled;
- which system is the source of truth for value and balance.

Do not copy another business’s fine print. Wisconsin’s Department of Agriculture, Trade and Consumer Protection explains that [federal rules provide safeguards for many retail and bank gift cards and for printed and electronic gift certificates](https://datcp.wi.gov/Pages/Publications/GiftCards428.aspx). Have the business’s payment provider, accountant, and qualified legal adviser review its specific product and promotion. This checklist is marketing guidance, not legal or accounting advice.

## Build one clear purchase page

A campaign is easier to measure and maintain when every channel points to one useful page. Include:

- a direct “Buy gift card” button near the top;
- available amounts or custom-value rules;
- physical versus electronic delivery choices;
- delivery timing and pickup details;
- redemption locations and eligible services;
- important terms in readable language;
- a contact path for delivery or balance problems;
- a short answer to common questions;
- a confirmation that works well on mobile.

Avoid making a customer search the site for a purchase button after clicking an ad or post. If the checkout is hosted by a payment provider, explain what will happen before sending the visitor to that provider.

For local discovery, keep the business name, address, phone, website, and hours consistent with the broader [Google Business Profile optimization checklist for southeastern Wisconsin](/blog/optimize-google-business-profile-southeastern-wisconsin/). A gift-card campaign should not introduce a second phone number, outdated location, or conflicting holiday schedule.

## Use Google Business Profile without treating a post as the checkout

Google says Business Profile posts can share [offers, updates, and event details on Search and Maps](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7342169). An Offer post can include dates, a coupon code, a link, and terms. Use that surface to summarize the campaign and link to the canonical purchase page.

The post should answer four questions quickly:

1. What is available?
2. Who might it suit?
3. When does the stated offer end?
4. Where can the customer buy it?

Do not rely on a graphic to carry essential terms. Put the key facts in accessible page and post text. Before Thanksgiving and again before late-December shopping, verify special hours using the [Wisconsin Google holiday-hours checklist](/blog/wisconsin-small-business-google-holiday-hours-checklist/).

## Automate reminders around customer intent, not pressure

Useful automation reduces missed steps while keeping a person accountable. A small business might configure:

- a purchase confirmation with delivery and support details;
- an internal alert when delivery fails or a high-value order needs review;
- a reminder to staff to refresh the Google post and homepage placement;
- a scheduled email to customers who have opted into marketing;
- suppression rules so purchasers do not receive an irrelevant “you forgot to buy” message;
- a January service reminder for recipients who have consented to future communication;
- a weekly report of sales, delivery exceptions, refunds, and redemptions.

Do not add gift recipients to marketing lists merely because someone sent them a card. Keep transactional delivery separate from promotional consent. Give a human owner access to failed-send logs, refund requests, and the ability to pause the sequence.

The same principle applies to campaign copy: AI can draft variants, but a person should verify the offer, dates, terms, links, and local details. The site’s guide to [human review in local AI workflows](/blog/ai-local-human-in-the-loop/) explains why the approval step matters.

## Train staff with a one-page answer sheet

Customers experience one business even when the systems are separate. Give front-desk, phone, sales, and service staff the same concise reference:

- campaign start and end dates;
- purchase URL and in-person options;
- delivery choices and expected timing;
- redemption process;
- balance lookup process;
- promotion terms;
- refund and lost-card escalation path;
- person responsible for system problems.

Test the phone and chat experience with a real question: “Can I buy a gift card today and schedule it to arrive next month?” The answer should match the checkout rather than a guess.

## Measure the journey, not only gross sales

Track a small set of operational measures that can lead to action:

- visits to the purchase page by channel;
- completed purchases;
- checkout or payment failures;
- delivery failures and support contacts;
- refund requests;
- cards and promotional bonuses redeemed;
- time between purchase and redemption;
- first-time recipient visits, only where the business can identify them appropriately;
- remaining balances handled according to the platform and applicable rules.

Use campaign parameters consistently on email, social, and Google Business Profile links. Reconcile marketing reports with the payment and gift-card systems before making revenue claims. A click is not a purchase, a purchase is not immediately earned revenue in every accounting context, and an unredeemed balance is not proof of customer satisfaction.

## A 60-minute prelaunch checklist

Before the next promotion goes live:

1. Confirm the gift-card product, eligible uses, delivery methods, and support owner.
2. Review the offer and terms with the appropriate business advisers.
3. Complete a test purchase, delivery, balance check, and redemption.
4. Check the purchase page on a phone and desktop.
5. Verify the receipt, gift email, sender identity, links, and reply path.
6. Point website, email, social, Google Business Profile, and signs to the same page.
7. Confirm current business and holiday hours across customer-facing channels.
8. Give staff the one-page answer sheet and test a phone inquiry.
9. Turn on delivery-failure and payment-exception alerts.
10. Schedule a January redemption review before launch day.

The goal is not to publish the loudest holiday offer. It is to make a local gift easy to discover, easy to buy, dependable to deliver, and pleasant to redeem.
