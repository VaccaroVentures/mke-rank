Wisconsin businesses should prepare one winter-weather decision process **before the first storm**, then publish the same verified status everywhere customers look. Name the decision owner, define what “open,” “delayed,” “remote,” and “closed” mean, write message templates, and connect the website, Google Business Profile, phone greeting, email or text system, and social channels. During an event, update those channels from one approved source of truth and include the next review time.

Late October is the right planning window. ReadyWisconsin says the [average date of Wisconsin’s first snowstorm is November 10](https://readywisconsin.wi.gov/winter-storms/), and the state’s [2026 public-awareness calendar](https://readywisconsin.wi.gov/resources/) lists Winter Weather Awareness Week for November 16–20. That does not predict a storm on either date. It does mean businesses have a short runway to test their process before winter disruptions become routine.

Public search behavior supports planning for sharp, event-driven attention rather than a smooth seasonal climb. In the [Google Trends view for “winter storm warning” in Wisconsin over the past five years](https://trends.google.com/trends/explore?date=today%205-y&geo=US-WI&q=winter%20storm%20warning), reviewed October 25, 2026, the normalized weekly index reached 82 in the week of December 18, 2022; 100 in the week of January 7, 2024; 28 in the week of February 9, 2025; and 45 in the week of November 23, 2025. The current partial week read 1. Google Trends values are relative interest, not search counts or forecasts. The useful lesson is operational: questions can arrive suddenly when a real storm develops, so build the communication system while attention is still low.

## The short answer: publish five facts in every winter update

Every public update should answer the same five questions:

1. **What is the current operating status?** Open, delayed, remote, limited, or closed.
2. **Which location or service area is affected?** Name the storefront, office, route zone, or county.
3. **What happens to existing appointments or orders?** Say whether the team will contact customers, whether pickup is available, or whether service is postponed.
4. **What can customers do now?** Give one monitored phone number, form, ordering link, or emergency direction.
5. **When will the next update appear?** Use a specific time and time zone, even if the status may remain unchanged.

A useful closure message might read: “Our Waukesha office is closed to walk-in customers today, Tuesday, because of hazardous travel. Scheduled customers will receive a direct update by 8:30 a.m. The phone team is available from 9 a.m. to 3 p.m. We will post the next operating update by 2 p.m. Central.”

Avoid “closed due to weather” by itself. It leaves customers guessing about appointments, deliveries, calls, refunds, and tomorrow’s hours.

## Build a decision ladder before the forecast becomes urgent

The National Weather Service distinguishes among outlooks, watches, advisories, and warnings. Its national guidance explains that a [winter storm watch means significant winter weather is possible, while a warning calls for action](https://www.weather.gov/safety/winter-ww). ReadyWisconsin similarly describes an outlook as a planning signal, a watch as time to prepare, an advisory as a travel-impact event, and a warning as dangerous weather that is expected or occurring.

Those products should inform a business decision, but they should not automatically dictate the same response for every company. A remote accounting firm, a childcare center, a restaurant, and a home-service fleet face different risks. Build a short ladder that combines official alerts with the conditions that matter to the operation.

- **Hazardous Weather Outlook:** Check staffing, inventory, routes, remote access, and vendor dependencies. Usually no public message is needed yet; prepare drafts.
- **Winter Storm Watch:** Confirm the decision owner and next review time, then contact critical staff. Share an advance notice only if customers need time to make plans.
- **Winter Weather Advisory:** Review travel, parking, delivery, and appointment impacts by location. Publish precise limitations if service changes.
- **Winter Storm Warning or unsafe local conditions:** Activate the approved closure, delay, or remote-work decision. Update every customer-facing channel and contact affected customers directly.
- **Conditions improve:** Verify entrances, roads, staffing, systems, and vendor access. Publish the reopening status and any remaining limitations.

Do not turn the ladder into an automated weather-to-closure switch. Official alerts cover broad areas, local conditions vary, and someone still needs authority to assess the actual workplace and approve the message.

## Choose one source of truth and one decision owner

Winter communication breaks down when a manager updates Facebook, a receptionist changes voicemail, and a third person edits Google with different times. Use one internal record for the current status. It can be a simple shared form or table, but it should contain:

- affected location or service area;
- approved status and effective time;
- reason stated in restrained, factual language;
- impact on appointments, orders, deliveries, and walk-ins;
- monitored customer contact path;
- next review time;
- approver name and timestamp;
- channels updated and confirmation that each update succeeded.

Assign a primary decision owner and a backup. Give channel access to the people who may actually be working during a storm, and test multifactor authentication before anyone is home on an icy morning.

This is a good candidate for [workflow and business automation](/services/workflow-business-automation/): one approved record can create channel-specific drafts, notify the team, and produce an update checklist. Keep a human approval step. Weather, staffing, and safety decisions are too contextual for an unsupervised system to publish.

## Update Google Business Profile without erasing normal hours

Customers often check Search or Maps before visiting. Google says [special hours are intended for brief changes such as special events or other exceptional circumstances](https://support.google.com/business/answer/6303076?gl=US&hl=en). They can be used for a temporary adjustment or a closure of up to six consecutive days; a closure of seven or more days, or one with no known end, should use the separate temporary-closure status.

For a one-day storm closure or delayed opening:

1. Set special hours for the affected date.
2. Check that the location’s main hours are already accurate; Google notes that special hours require the profile to be open with main hours.
3. Add a concise update post with the service impact and next review time. Google’s [Business Profile post guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7342169?hl=en) allows updates with a description, media, and an action link.
4. Verify the public profile from a signed-out or ordinary customer view.
5. Update or remove stale information when the business reopens.

Do not replace normal weekly hours with a storm schedule and hope someone remembers to undo it. Do not mark a business temporarily closed for a single snow day. ## Put the website notice where customers can act on it

A website alert should appear on the pages customers use during the disruption, not only on the homepage. A restaurant may need the notice on ordering and location pages. A contractor may need it on service, booking, and contact pages. A clinic or appointment-based business may need it beside the scheduling flow.

The notice should include:

- the current status and effective time;
- the exact location, department, or service area affected;
- what happens to booked appointments, pickups, or deliveries;
- a monitored contact or self-service action;
- the next update time;
- the original publish time and latest revision time.

Use a visible banner or status component that works at narrow mobile widths. Avoid a modal that blocks emergency details, and do not rely on an image containing the only copy; customers and assistive technology need real text.

## Contact affected customers before broadcasting widely

Public updates matter, but a customer with an 8 a.m. appointment should not have to discover a closure on social media. Build a priority order:

1. Employees and contractors who need to travel or open the location.
2. Customers with same-day appointments, pickups, deliveries, or reservations.
3. Vendors, landlords, or partners whose access or timing changes.
4. The broader public through the website, Google, email, and social channels.

Segment direct messages by the action customers need to take. Someone whose appointment will be rescheduled needs different information from someone whose order is ready for curbside pickup. A home-service customer needs to know whether the technician is delayed, whether dispatch will call, and whether the company truly provides emergency service.

Do not use a weather update as cover for a promotional blast. Send operational information to the people affected, use only channels for which the business has appropriate permission, and make replies reach a monitored person or queue.

## Make phone, chat, and AI answers match the public status

The phone greeting, call center, chat widget, and AI assistant should read from the same approved status. Give them explicit rules for:

- today’s opening and closing times;
- whether staff can reschedule an appointment;
- which services are paused or still operating;
- which locations or routes are affected;
- how urgent requests are escalated;
- when a human will respond;
- what the system must not promise.

An automated answer should never invent a reopening time or claim a technician is “on the way” without dispatch confirmation. If the current status cannot be loaded, fall back to a safe message that directs the customer to a monitored channel instead of guessing. The broader principle is the same one in [AI for local SEO: keep humans in the loop](/blog/ai-local-human-in-the-loop/).

## Separate safety information from business promotion

Link to authoritative weather and travel information rather than rewriting forecasts. ReadyWisconsin and the National Weather Service should be the source for public hazard language. The business update should explain the business decision: whether the location is open, which services are available, and what customers should do.

Remove claims such as:

- “Roads are safe now.” A business is not the road authority.
- “The storm will end by noon.” Link to the current official forecast instead.
- “All appointments will be on time.” Do not promise what routing cannot confirm.
- “We are open as usual” when delivery, parking, entrances, or staffing are limited.
- “Emergency service available” if calls only go to voicemail.

For multi-county operators, do not apply one Milwaukee status to Kenosha, Waukesha, or Lake Geneva without checking each route and location. Link updates to the correct [southeastern Wisconsin location page](/locations/) so customers can identify the coverage that applies to them.

## Use a two-message reopening sequence

“Open again” may still be incomplete. Reopening has two stages:

1. **Internal verification:** Confirm staffing, safe access, parking or loading areas, systems, inventory, and route conditions. Reconcile delayed orders and appointments.
2. **Customer update:** State the reopening time, remaining limitations, how displaced appointments will be handled, and when normal service is expected.

Keep the closure notice’s revision history long enough for the team to resolve disputes or missed appointments. Remove stale website banners and social pinning, restore the phone greeting, confirm Google hours, and close the internal incident record.

## A 60-minute pre-winter communication test

Run this exercise before the first significant snow:

1. Pick one realistic scenario: a 10 a.m. delayed opening at the Milwaukee-area office with field routes reviewed separately.
2. Have the backup decision owner approve a test status.
3. Draft the website, Google, phone, direct-customer, staff, and social versions from that status.
4. Confirm every version contains the same operating facts and next update time.
5. Preview the website banner on a phone.
6. Verify that same-day customer lists can be filtered without exposing unrelated customer information.
7. Test that replies reach a monitored inbox, phone queue, or staff member.
8. Check account access and backup authentication methods.
9. Ask a staff member who did not write the messages to explain what customers should do.
10. Record gaps, assign owners, and repeat the test after fixes.

The best winter-weather communication is not dramatic. It is fast, consistent, specific, and easy to revise. Wisconsin businesses that decide roles, templates, and channels in late October can spend storm day serving customers and protecting staff instead of debating who should change the voicemail.
